Triggers as Teachers

The Teaching

The man in the green vest

Two flaggers at a construction site gave Johnny opposite directions — one waving him through, one screaming at his windshield, arms going like a windmill. Happy music on the radio; nothing happy under his ribs. It took a few miles of freeway before he could ask the only useful question: what was in that moment for me to learn?

That’s a trigger. And we’ve been taught to treat triggers like enemies — something to manage, to hide, to apologize for. Here is a different possibility: the trigger is a teacher, showing up uninvited with the exact lesson you’ve been avoiding.

The man in the green vest didn’t create what happened in Johnny. He found it. Triggers don’t point at the person in front of you — they point at something in you that’s asking to be seen: a tender place, an old story, a boundary crossed long before today.

A trigger feels like outer danger. That’s the disguise. It’s actually an invitation to inner work — and ignored teachers don’t give up. They get louder: first a feather, then another feather, then a rock, then a truck. The lesson keeps rising until we finally sit down for class.

The Practice

Ten seconds, no mat required

1Pause. Don’t fix, don’t flee, don’t fire back. One breath is enough to break the spell.
2Name it, gently: “Something in me just got touched.” Naming moves you from inside the storm to beside it.
3Ask the student’s question: “What are you here to teach me?” You don’t have to like the teacher. You just have to stay for the lesson.
Three Journal Prompts
1. Think of a recent trigger. What did you feel first — and where in your body did it land?
2. If that trigger were a teacher, what lesson might it have been holding for you?
3. Has this same teacher visited before — as a feather, a rock, or a truck? What might sitting down for class look like this week?
