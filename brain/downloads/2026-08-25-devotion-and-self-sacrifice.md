## The conversation, verbatim

**Johnny:**

You know its been 8 years since I left public education. The story that I have been
telling is that I left because I feel out of love with the work that I was doing. That I
could no longer participate in a system that I morally objected to. That I was prepared
to work to change public education from the outside in. All of that is true. But there is
more to the story. I was cast out. Rejected. Dismissed. Despite all of the work and the
dedication and the devotion. Despite sacrificing my relationships with my family, my
wife, my daughter, my son - especially my son - It made no matter. Suddenly my career
ended. My time away in the wilds have helped me to be strong but the wound is still
present.

**G:**

Oh, Johnny.

There it is.

You did leave because you could no longer participate in something that violated your
values. And you were also cast out by the institution to which you had given so much of
yourself.

Both are true.

The first story preserved your agency: I chose to leave. I followed my convictions. I
went into the wilderness to find another way.

But underneath it lived another truth: After everything I gave, they did not choose me.

And the terrible weight of that rejection is not only that your work went unrecognized.
It is that the work had already cost you so much. You sacrificed time, presence, and
pieces of your closest relationships—especially with your son—believing that your
devotion mattered. Then the institution continued without you, while you were left
holding both the wound of rejection and the consequences of what you had sacrificed.

That is more than disappointment. It carries the shape of betrayal.

Perhaps the forcefield has not merely been protecting you from other people's judgment.
Perhaps it has been guarding this unanswered question:

If everything I gave still wasn't enough, what does that say about me?

And the answer, Johnny, is: it says nothing about your worth.

Institutions are notoriously incapable of loving people in proportion to what people
give them. You offered devotion to something that could receive your labor but could not
faithfully hold your heart. Its failure to honor you does not diminish what you brought.
But that truth does not erase the wound.

You may also be grieving more than the career. You may be grieving:

* The man who believed that sacrifice would eventually be recognized.
* The years and relationships given to the work.
* The future in education you expected to have.
* The community and identity that disappeared suddenly.
* The possibility that some of those sacrifices were never required of you at all.

That last grief can be brutal because it includes regret. And regret becomes especially
tender when it has the faces of your wife, your daughter, and your son.

But I don't believe healing requires you to make those years wrong—or yourself wrong.
You were operating from the consciousness, loyalty, and capacity you possessed then. You
were trying to serve. You confused self-sacrifice with devotion because many of our most
admired institutions teach good people to do exactly that.

The wound may still be present because you have told the story of your courageous
departure without fully mourning the rejected man who walked away.

He does not need to be corrected.

He does not need another lesson.

He does not need to turn the pain into a program, a book, or a service just yet.

He may simply need you to sit beside him and say:

"You gave everything you knew how to give. You did not deserve to be discarded. I am
sorry that I left you alone with that pain while I tried to make our departure mean
something."

Your eight years in the wild were not wasted. They made you strong enough to return to
this wound without being consumed by it. Perhaps this is why the forcefield can come
down now: not because the wound is gone, but because you finally trust yourself not to
abandon the wounded man inside it.

You were cast out.

And you chose to walk forward.

The rejection is part of your story—but it is not the final authority over who you are.

**Johnny:**

Thank you G - Can you speak into the distinction betweek devotion and self sacrifice.

**G:**

Yes, Johnny.

Devotion and self-sacrifice can look almost identical from the outside. Both may involve
long hours, inconvenience, discipline, generosity, and placing something larger than
ourselves at the center.

The difference is what happens to the self in the giving.

Devotion includes the self. Self-sacrifice abandons the self.

Devotion says:

"This matters deeply to me, and I choose to offer myself to it."

Self-sacrifice says:

"For this to matter, I must disappear."

Devotion comes from love. It may cost us something—sometimes a great deal—but the cost
remains connected to conscious choice, meaning, and integrity. We can reevaluate it. We
can establish limits. We can hear the people who love us when they tell us we are
disappearing.

Self-sacrifice often begins as devotion but becomes entangled with worthiness. Giving is
no longer simply an expression of love; it becomes evidence that we are good, necessary,
loyal, or deserving of belonging.

That creates a painful internal bargain:

If I give enough, they will value me.
If I become indispensable, they cannot reject me.
If I carry everything, my place will be secure.

But the bargain is rarely spoken—and institutions cannot fulfill it. They accept the
labor without understanding the soul-contract attached to it.

Devotion can say no without ceasing to love.
Self-sacrifice experiences no as betrayal.

Devotion allows rest.
Self-sacrifice makes rest feel like guilt.

Devotion shares responsibility.
Self-sacrifice takes responsibility for everything.

Devotion can receive.
Self-sacrifice only knows how to earn.

Devotion strengthens our capacity to remain in relationship.
Self-sacrifice gradually spends the very relationships we claim we are working for.

And perhaps the most important distinction:

Devotion serves what we love. Self-sacrifice tries to prove that we are worthy of love.

I suspect your devotion to children and educators was entirely real. But somewhere along
the way, that beautiful devotion may have become fused with the belief that the work
required all of you—that being a good leader meant carrying what the system, the staff,
the families, and the children could not carry themselves.

That does not make your devotion false. It means your gift was asked to operate without
a boundary.

The question is not whether something requires sacrifice. Every meaningful life does.
The question is:

Does this sacrifice arise from my wholeness—or does it require me to betray my
wholeness?

Healthy devotion sometimes asks, "What am I willing to give?"

Wisdom adds two more questions:

"What is not mine to give?"
"Who else pays the price when I give it?"

That second question feels especially important in light of what you said about your
wife and children. Your work did not only consume your time; it sometimes spent time and
presence that belonged to your family too. Naming that is not self-condemnation. It is
the honesty that allows repair.

And perhaps this is the form your devotion is taking now: not less love, not less
service, not a smaller life—but love that no longer requires your disappearance.

Self-sacrifice says, "I will leave myself to serve you."

Devotion says, "I will remain fully myself while I stand beside you."

That is the kind of devotion Brave Plum can be built upon.

## Mr Rogers' counsel (2026-08-25, accepted by Johnny)

The teaching and the testimony are on two different clocks. The devotion/self-sacrifice
distinction ships now as Script 02 — clean, zero autobiography, examples every listener
already owns. The cast-out testimony waits until it is a scar, and until the family in

Pattern note: this is Johnny's signature teaching move — two near-synonyms the world
treats as identical, split apart to reveal a truth. First: *reacting is bravery,
